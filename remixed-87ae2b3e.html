Exploring World Religions

A comparative study of Christianity, Islam, and Hinduism — examining their beliefs, ethics, key figures, and sacred rituals.

✝ Christianity ☪ Islam 🕉 Hinduism

Topic 1

The Image & Nature of God

How do these three religions understand and describe the divine? Each offers a distinct vision of ultimate reality — yet all three point humanity toward a transcendent source.

✝

Christianity

Abrahamic · Monotheistic

Nature of God

Christianity is monotheistic, believing in one God who exists as the Holy Trinity: God the Father, God the Son (Jesus Christ), and God the Holy Spirit. God is omnipotent, omniscient, omnipresent, loving, merciful, and just — the creator and sustainer of all existence.

Image of God

Humans are made in God's image and hold inherent dignity and worth. God is personal, desiring a relationship with humanity. Jesus Christ is understood as God incarnate, fully revealing God's nature to the world.

Key Sources

The Bible — Genesis, The Gospels

☪

Islam

Abrahamic · Strictly Monotheistic

Nature of God

Islam teaches strict monotheism. Allah has no partners, equals, or offspring and is all-powerful, all-knowing, merciful, compassionate, and the creator of everything. The absolute oneness of God is Tawhid.

Image of God

Allah is transcendent and beyond full human comprehension. Muslims do not create images of God. Allah communicates through prophets; Muhammad is the final and seal of all prophets.

Key Sources

The Qur'an · Hadith

🕉

Hinduism

Dharmic · Non-Abrahamic

Nature of God

Hinduism teaches that ultimate reality is Brahman — an eternal, infinite, supreme spiritual force present in all things and the source of all creation. Brahman may be worshipped through many deities.

Image of God

Major deities include Brahma (creator), Vishnu (preserver), and Shiva (destroyer), each representing an aspect of Brahman. Every person holds an eternal soul — Atman — intrinsically connected to Brahman.

Key Sources

Vedas · Upanishads · Bhagavad Gita

⚖ Comparison — The Divine Across Three Traditions

Christianity and Islam are both Abrahamic and strictly monotheistic, agreeing that one all-powerful God created the universe. However, Christianity introduces the doctrine of the Trinity — God as three persons in one — while Islam insists God is absolutely singular and indivisible.

Hinduism differs fundamentally by presenting Brahman as ultimate reality accessible through multiple divine manifestations. While Christians believe Jesus is God incarnate and Muslims honour Muhammad as the final prophet, Hinduism focuses on the soul's (Atman's) journey back to Brahman.

Despite differences, all three traditions affirm a supreme spiritual reality, offer guidance for human life, and emphasise a deep connection between humanity and the divine.

Topic 2

Moral & Ethical Principles

Sacred texts across all three traditions call believers to justice, compassion, and respect for human dignity. Here we examine core moral teachings alongside primary-source evidence.

✝

Christianity

Love · Justice · Forgiveness

Core Moral Teachings

Christian morality centres on love, compassion, justice, forgiveness, and service. Believers are called to follow Jesus's example by treating others with kindness and respect, and actively defending the oppressed.

Sacred Text Evidence

"Learn to do right; seek justice. Defend the oppressed." Isaiah 1:17
"There is neither Jew nor Gentile, neither slave nor free, nor male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus." Galatians 3:28

Meaning

These passages champion equality and justice for all people. Christians are urged to dismantle oppressive systems and build communities rooted in fairness and dignity.

☪

Islam

Honesty · Charity · Respect

Core Moral Teachings

Islam teaches honesty, justice, compassion, charity, respect, and responsibility. Muslims strive to follow Allah's guidance as expressed in the Qur'an and the example of the Prophet in the Hadith.

Sacred Text Evidence

"We created you from a male and female and made you peoples and tribes so that you may know one another." Qur'an 49:13

Meaning

This passage affirms the equality of all human beings regardless of race, nationality, or social class. It calls Muslims to mutual understanding, respect, and community across all divides.

🕉

Hinduism

Dharma · Ahimsa · Karma

Core Moral Teachings

Hindu ethics rest on Dharma (duty/righteousness), Ahimsa (non-violence), truthfulness, self-discipline, and compassion. Actions generate karma — consequences that shape future lives.

Sacred Text Evidence

The Bhagavad Gita teaches that individuals must perform their duties selflessly, acting from righteousness rather than personal gain. Bhagavad Gita

Meaning

These teachings call believers to live responsibly, refrain from harming any living being, and contribute positively to the world — motivated by righteousness, not self-interest.

⚖ Comparison — Shared Ethics, Distinct Paths

All three religions promote compassion, respect, justice, and service to others. While the languages and traditions differ, the ethical cores are remarkably convergent: a call to treat every human being with dignity.

Christianity emphasises love and forgiveness channelled through the life and teachings of Jesus. Islam focuses on obedience to Allah, expressed through justice, charity, and care for the community. Hinduism frames ethics through Dharma and karma, stressing non-violence and selfless duty.

Despite originating in different sacred texts and cultural contexts, all three invite followers to build a more just, compassionate, and peaceful world.

Topic 3

Key Figures

Who shaped these traditions? The founders, prophets, and reformers at the heart of Christianity, Islam, and Hinduism continue to guide billions of believers today.

✝ Christianity

Jesus of Nazareth

c. 4 BCE – c. 30 CE · Founder / Central Figure

Christians believe Jesus is the Son of God and the Messiah. His teachings on love, forgiveness, and the Kingdom of God formed the foundation of Christianity. Though crucified, his followers proclaimed his resurrection — the defining event of the faith. Jesus did not set out to found a new religion so much as to renew the covenant between God and humanity; yet his life became the axis of a world religion. He remains central to Christian worship, theology, and ethics today.

✝ Christianity

Paul of Tarsus (St. Paul)

c. 5 CE – c. 64 CE · Apostle & Theologian

A former persecutor of Christians who converted after a vision of the risen Christ, Paul became the most prolific writer of the New Testament. His letters defined core Christian theology — grace, justification by faith, and the universal mission of the Church. Paul brought the gospel to the Greco-Roman world, transforming a Jewish sect into a global movement. His intellectual contributions still shape Christian doctrine across every denomination.

☪ Islam

Prophet Muhammad ﷺ

570 – 632 CE · Final Prophet

Muslims believe Muhammad received the final revelation of God's word — the Qur'an — through the angel Jibril. He unified the Arabian Peninsula under Islam and established the foundational practices of the faith. Muhammad did not claim divinity but is revered as the perfect model of human conduct. His sayings and actions (the Hadith and Sunnah) serve as a guide for Muslim life in every era. His legacy underpins Islamic law, spirituality, and community to this day.

☪ Islam

Imam Ali ibn Abi Talib

601 – 661 CE · Companion & Fourth Caliph

The cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet, Ali is revered across Islam and is especially central in Shia Islam. Known for his wisdom, justice, and piety, Ali's governance and writings continue to inspire Islamic jurisprudence and spirituality. His Nahj al-Balagha (Peak of Eloquence) remains a landmark of Islamic literature. His life raises enduring questions about leadership, justice, and the nature of religious authority that Muslims wrestle with today.

🕉 Hinduism

Adi Shankaracharya

c. 788 – 820 CE · Philosopher & Reformer

Shankara consolidated Hindu philosophy through his school of Advaita Vedanta — the non-dualistic teaching that Atman and Brahman are ultimately one. He travelled across India debating rival schools and revitalising Vedic tradition at a time when it was in decline. Shankara established four major monastic centres that still exist today. His commentaries on the Upanishads, Bhagavad Gita, and Brahma Sutras remain the bedrock of Hindu philosophical study worldwide.

🕉 Hinduism

Mahatma Gandhi

1869 – 1948 · Leader, Activist & Contemporary Figure

Deeply rooted in Hindu values — especially Ahimsa (non-violence) and Satya (truth) — Gandhi led India's independence movement using nonviolent civil disobedience. He demonstrated that religious ethics could be a powerful political force. Gandhi's methods influenced civil rights movements worldwide, from Martin Luther King Jr. to Nelson Mandela. He shows how ancient Hindu teachings can remain urgently relevant in addressing injustice in the modern world.

Topic 4

Rituals & Rites of Passage

Rituals mark the sacred moments of human life — birth, commitment, and death. Each religion has developed rich ceremonies that have evolved over centuries while preserving their spiritual core.

✝ Christianity

Initiation

Baptism

Elements: Water used to symbolize purification and rebirth into the Christian community.
Evolution: Originally an adult rite of entry; many denominations later adopted infant baptism.
Meaning: Forgiveness of sins, spiritual rebirth, and commitment to God.

Maturity

Confirmation

Elements: Public affirmation of faith with a blessing from church leaders.
Evolution: Developed into a distinct sacrament as the Church grew and spread.
Meaning: Spiritual maturity and a deeper commitment to Christian beliefs.

Union

Marriage

Elements: Vows, prayers, witnesses, and a lifelong commitment made before God.
Evolution: Adapted across cultures and denominations throughout history.
Meaning: Love, unity, faithfulness, and lifelong partnership.

☪ Islam

Declaration

Shahadah

Elements: Declaration that there is no god but Allah and Muhammad is His messenger.
Evolution: Unchanged since the earliest period of Islam.
Meaning: Commitment to Islam and belief in the oneness of Allah.

Daily Worship

Salah (Prayer)

Elements: Five daily prayers facing Mecca, performed at prescribed times.
Evolution: Standardized during Islam's early development; consistent worldwide.
Meaning: Strengthens the believer's bond with Allah and instills discipline.

Annual

Ramadan

Elements: A month of fasting from sunrise to sunset, combined with prayer and charity.
Evolution: Modern life has changed daily routines, but the sacred purpose is unchanged.
Meaning: Self-discipline, spiritual growth, gratitude, and empathy for those in need.

🕉 Hinduism

Birth

Namkaran (Naming Ceremony)

Elements: A ceremony welcoming a newborn into the family and religious community.
Evolution: Practices vary widely by region and cultural tradition.
Meaning: Celebrates the child's identity and the beginning of their spiritual journey.

Union

Vivaha (Marriage)

Elements: Prayers, sacred fire rituals, exchange of vows, and seven steps around the holy fire.
Evolution: Modern weddings often blend traditional rites with contemporary customs.
Meaning: Sacred union of two individuals and their families before the divine.

Death

Antyeshti (Funeral Rites)

Elements: Cremation, prayers, and ceremonies performed by family members.
Evolution: Practices have adapted across different countries while retaining core beliefs.
Meaning: Guides the soul on its spiritual journey and honors the deceased.

⚖ Comparison — Rituals Across Traditions

All three religions use rituals to mark the key stages of human life and deepen believers' connection to the divine. Christianity emphasises sacraments like baptism and confirmation. Islam's worship life is structured around the Five Pillars, including the Shahadah, Salah, and Ramadan. Hinduism anchors life's transitions through the Samskaras — life-cycle rites from birth to death.

Over centuries, these rituals have adapted to changing societies, cultures, and technologies. Modern Hindu weddings incorporate contemporary elements; Ramadan is observed by Muslims using apps and social media to track prayer times. Yet the core purposes remain constant: expressing faith, strengthening community, preserving tradition, and connecting with the transcendent.

For believers, rituals provide identity, belonging, and spiritual meaning — anchoring the individual within a community that stretches across generations.
